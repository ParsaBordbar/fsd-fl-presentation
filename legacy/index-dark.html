3 · The proposed solution

Reverse the pipeline

Centralized ML: move the data to the computation.

Federated learning: move the computation to the data.

The one-liner is what people remember. Say it slowly. You don't upload your records: you receive the model, improve it locally, send back a summary of what changed.

3 · The proposed solution · the original announcement

Google, April 2017

"Your device downloads the current model, improves it by learning from data on your phone, and then summarizes the changes as a small focused update. Only this update to the model is sent to the cloud … where it is immediately averaged with other user updates to improve the shared model."McMahan & Ramage · Google AI Blog · the Gboard keyboard was the first deployment

  1. Download the current model to the device
  2. Improve it from local data — the phone does the training
  3. Summarize the change as a small, focused update
  4. Send only the update, averaged immediately with everyone else's
Let the Google description do the concrete work. This is the same keyboard from the hook — the original public deployment was Gboard's suggestion model.

4 · How it works · one round

Steps 0–2: the model goes out

  1. Initialize the global model — random, or from a checkpoint. Identical to centralized.
  2. Send it to selected client nodes. Not all — selecting more and more has diminishing returns. Everyone selected starts from the same parameters.
  3. Train locally. Each hospital trains on its own data — not to convergence, just a little: one epoch, or a few mini-batches.
  4. Send updates back
  5. Aggregate
  6. Repeat
The central diagram — server in the middle, three hospitals around it. Reused on the next slide with the arrows reversed. Stress step 2: local training is brief on purpose.
1.5 min

4 · How it works · one round

Steps 3–5: the updates come back

  1. Initialize
  2. Send to selected clients
  3. Train locally
  4. Send updates back. Each hospital now holds a slightly different model, because each saw different examples. Those differences go back — full parameters or accumulated gradients. Updates, not data.
  5. Aggregate. A hundred slightly different models; the server needs one. Combine them into a new global model.
  6. Repeat. That's one round. Everyone trained only briefly, so the global model has seen everyone's data — but only a little. Repeat until convergence.
Same diagram, arrows reversed. Land on "updates, not data" and move on — aggregation gets its own slide.

4 · How it works · aggregation

FedAvg — and why it is weighted

Federated Averaging (McMahan et al., 2017): average the updates, weighted by how many examples each client trained on.

wglobal ← Σk ( nk / n ) · wk    ·    nk = examples at client k,   n = Σ nk
Unweighted

Client A has 10 examples, client B has 100. A plain average gives both models the same say — so each of A's 10 examples influences the global model ten times as much as each of B's 100.

Weighted

A's model counts 10/110, B's counts 100/110. Now every data example has the same say, whichever client it lives on.

Say the 10-vs-100 explanation exactly as written. It's the clearest justification that exists and needs no notation. The formula is there for the people who want it.

4 · How it works · two things you get for free

The pattern generalizes

Federated evaluation

Measure the model across decentralized data the same way you trained it. Each hospital tests the global model on its own held-out notes and reports only the score.

A: 91% · B: 88% · C: 93% — per-site accuracy, no test records shipped.

Federated analytics

Answer a statistical question across many clients without collecting their answers. "What is the average typing speed across a million phones?" Each phone sends one number; usually combined with secure aggregation so the server sees only the total.

The cleanest bridge into privacy — next part.

5 · Limitations · it is not automatically private

Read the promise carefully

"Your data never leaves your device."every federated learning press release

True

It is a claim about raw records. The records really do stay.

But

The update leaves. And the update is computed from your data, by a known procedure, using a model the server chose and sent you.

So the natural question: can the update be run backwards?

Set up the reversal question cleanly. The three italics matter: from your data, known procedure, server-chosen model. Each one is a handle for the attack on the next slide.
2 min

5 · Limitations · gradient inversion

Deep Leakage from Gradients · Zhu, Liu & Han · NeurIPS 2019

  • Pixel-accurate for images, token-accurate for text — the recovered guess is the training data.
  • Geiping et al. 2020 (Inverting Gradients): better method, breaks already-trained networks at higher resolution.
  • NIST's blog series shows recovered training images side by side with the originals — look up Privacy Attacks in Federated Learning.
The most important slide. Walk the loop: blank guess → same model → compare the update it produces with the one the client actually sent → adjust → repeat. When they match, the guess IS the training data. Then give the caveat before anyone else does — next slide.
1 min

5 · Limitations · the honest caveat, and its limit

Curious server vs malicious server

Honest-but-curious

Follows the protocol, peeks at what it receives. Reconstruction gets much harder with:

  • larger batches (many records blur together)
  • more local training steps before sending
  • smaller, compressed updates

In a realistic large deployment, recovering one specific person's data is difficult.

Malicious

Remember who picks the model and the starting weights it hands you. A malicious server picks ones that make inversion easy — the "difficult" conditions were never the client's to choose.

"When the Curious Abandon Honesty: Federated Learning Is Not Private"Boenisch et al. · the title says it plainly

Concede the caveat first — big batches, lots of local steps, it's hard. Then: "difficult" describes a curious server. The server chooses the model. Cite the Boenisch title directly.

5 · Limitations · a second, separate leak

The model leaks too

Even with every update perfectly protected — training produces a model, and the model gets distributed.

Membership inference · Shokri et al. · IEEE S&P 2017

From the model's behaviour alone, determine whether a specific record was in its training set. Models are more confident on what they've seen — and that confidence is measurable from outside.

Generic image classifier: a curiosity. So what if your cat photo was in the training set.

Model trained on a psychiatric hospital's records: "was this person in the training set?" — that is the secret.

Flat delivery. Don't dramatize. The example does the work.

5 · The real protections · input privacy

Secure aggregation: the masks cancel

Every pair of participants secretly agrees on a random number. One adds it to their update, the other subtracts it. Individually unreadable — summed, every mask cancels and the total is exact.

HospitalReal updatePairwise masksWhat the server sees
A5+ rAB(7) + rAC(2)14
B3− rAB(7) + rBC(5)1
C4− rAC(2) − rBC(5)−3
Sum12every r appears once +, once −12

The server learns the sum — 12 — and nothing else. It cannot tell that A's real value was 5. Bonawitz et al., Practical Secure Aggregation, 2017.

Dropouts are the hard part. The protocol lets the group recover a departed participant's mask, while the server can never unmask one still present. Flower ships a runnable example that logs each protocol stage.

The cancellation idea delights any audience — give it a moment. Walk the table: 14 + 1 − 3 = 12 = 5 + 3 + 4. Each mask appears once positive and once negative. Then the dropout point, briefly.

5 · The real protections · output privacy

Differential privacy: bounded influence

  1. Clip — limit how much any one participant can move the model
  2. Add noise — calibrated to that limit, so no single contribution is distinguishable
  3. Track the budget — every round spends privacy; the total across all rounds is what matters

McMahan et al., Learning Differentially Private Recurrent Language Models, ICLR 2018 — DP for the keyboard model. Flower wraps any strategy with fixed or adaptive clipping and an accountant.

Two questions decide whether it means anything

What is the unit of privacy?

One training example — one note, one message? Or everything one person contributed? A patient with fifty notes is protected fifty times less under example-level DP.

Who adds the noise?

A server you must trust (central DP) — or the participants themselves before sending (local DP)? Only one of those protects you from the server.

Unit and noise-adder are the two questions that expose a hollow DP claim. They return verbatim on the audit checklist in part 7.

5 · Limitations · the ordinary engineering limits

Even honest, even private

Non-identical data

Hospital A sees hearts, B sees children. Local models pull in different directions; the average lands somewhere neither hospital would choose. That was the plateau on the chart. (Hsu, Qi & Brown give the standard way to simulate this.)

Update size

A model update is hundreds of megabytes per client per round. Multiply by rounds. Bandwidth, not compute, is often the bottleneck.

Stragglers

Devices disconnect mid-round. Fast clients wait for slow ones. The round is as slow as the phone on the worst connection.

Fairness

Excellent average accuracy can mean bad accuracy for the small hospital, a minority dialect, a rare condition — because weighted averaging weights communities by how much data they brought.

The fairness card sets up part 7: weighting by data volume is a governance decision wearing the costume of a default. Point back at the demo table — Hospital A, 100 notes, 52% after ten rounds while the average reads 86%.

5 · Limitations · one more cost, stated plainly

There is no free privacy

Turn the privacy noise up and accuracy comes down. Anyone who says otherwise hasn't turned the knob.

  • Left of the curve: guarantee vacuous, model excellent
  • Right of the curve: guarantee strong, model useless
  • The setting is a choice somebody makes — and reports, or doesn't

Demo run · global accuracy after 10 rounds vs DP noise multiplier · clip 0.5 · IID

Measured on the demo (python fedavg_numpy.py --iid --noise σ): 99% at σ=0, 65% at the σ=1.0 Flower's docs call strong privacy, 37% at σ=4 where chance is 33%. On the non-IID case mix the collapse starts at σ=0.25. The exact curve depends on model and data; the shape does not. The point is that the knob exists and someone sets it. Bridge: "everything so far assumed good faith."

6 · Attacks on federated learning

Participants have write access to the model

Centralized ML: you own all the data, so nobody can poison it. Federated learning: every participant can push the shared model. A category of attack that simply doesn't exist when you hold the pile.

Data poisoning

Corrupt your local training data so your update pulls the model somewhere useful to you.
e.g. a hospital mislabels one diagnosis as another.

Model poisoning

Skip the data. Submit a crafted update directly — you control the bytes that leave your machine.

Sybil

One attacker presents as many participants to multiply its share of the average.
e.g. fifty fake phones, one operator.

Byzantine

Arbitrary or broken updates — malicious, or just a buggy client sending NaNs. The average doesn't care why.

Frame it as structural, not a bug: the thing that makes FL possible — accepting updates from parties you don't control — is what creates this category.

6 · Defences · why this isn't solved

The defences conflict

⚠ Secure aggregation

exists precisely so the server cannot see individual updates.

⚠ Robust aggregation

works by examining individual updates and discarding suspicious ones.

You usually cannot have both at full strength.

And a subtler version of the same tension: under genuinely non-identical data, an honest minority client looks statistically a lot like an attacker. Outlier rejection risks discarding exactly the participants you federated in order to include — the small hospital, the rare dialect.

So "we use federated learning with privacy and security protections" isn't a claim. It's a category. The claim is in the settings.

The conflict is the honest answer to "why isn't this solved". The honest-minority line ties robustness back to fairness. The callout is the thesis of the whole second half — say it, then go to the phone.

Discussion · the two positions, if the room needs seeding

Take it, or refuse it

Take it

The data still never moves. Secure aggregation means they see only the sum, not any institution's contribution. Refusing free infrastructure over a hypothetical is how community projects die of exhaustion while the proprietary alternative ships. Purity that produces nothing protects nobody.

Refuse it

Whoever runs the aggregator chooses who participates, which model is trained, what the privacy settings actually are, when to change them, and who receives the final weights. They can turn secure aggregation off and tell you afterwards — or not. You'd have distributed the data and centralized every decision, and handed the party with the most to gain the ability to make them — while keeping the appearance of a community project.

Take it — with the right to fork

Publish the protocol. Keep the federation runnable by anyone. Then the offer is a service, not a dependency. Whether that's a real safeguard or a comforting story depends on whether anyone actually maintains the ability to leave.

Notice what the argument was about. It wasn't cryptography — every technical protection in the offer was already on. The disagreement was about who gets to change the settings.

7 · Data freedom and free software

The claim is in the settings

A company can truthfully say "we use federated learning with secure aggregation and differential privacy" while:

The settingWhat makes it vacuousCompatible with the press release?

Nothing on that list is a lie. Every item makes the guarantee meaningless. From outside, you cannot tell a real guarantee from a vocabulary word.

The punchline. If the discussion produced the "who changes the settings" point, say "which is what [name] just said."

7 · How to audit a privacy claim · the slide to photograph

You cannot verify a promise you are not allowed to inspect

  1. Is the client free software? It is the code on your device deciding what leaves it.
  2. Is the aggregation code published? It decides what the server can see.
  3. Is secure aggregation on — and how many participants before anything is unmasked?
  4. Differential privacy: what unit, what budget, totalled over the whole run?
  5. Who runs the aggregator, and who receives the final model?
  6. Can you fork it? Your own federation, your own aggregator, settings your community chose.
Open with the callback: "an hour ago, none of us could answer the phone question. Here's the answer — these are the six things you'd need." Not rhetoric: a description of the mechanism. If the client isn't free software, "the data never leaves" is an assertion, not a fact. The freedom to fork turns a promise from something you're given into something you hold — exactly what the discussion landed on.

7 · An open argument, directly relevant

What does "open source AI" even mean?

OSI · Open Source AI Definition 1.0 · Oct 2024

Requires source code, model parameters, and a description of the training data detailed enough to build a substantially equivalent system — but not the data itself.

FSF · working group on ML freedom

The stricter line: if the training data isn't free, the application isn't free. Weights without the data are a binary you can't rebuild.

I won't resolve that. I'll point out that federated learning is a concrete case where the data genuinely cannot be shipped — which is exactly the situation they're arguing about.

Give both, don't pick. FL is the case where "publish the data" is not an option even in principle — that's why it belongs in this argument.

Not solved by the architecture
  • Copyright — whose text, on what licence
  • Consent — did the patient agree to this
  • Dialect fairness — weighted averaging still weights by volume
  • Who holds the evaluation set — whoever scores the model steers it

Those are decisions people make. The architecture puts them in reach of the people producing the data.

The architecture won't make copyright, consent or fairness decisions for you. What it does is put those decisions in reach of the people producing the data.

The question isn't only whether AI can learn without moving our data. It's whether the people producing that data can inspect, modify, govern and fork what gets built from it.

Likely questions

So is federated learning useless for privacy?
No. It removes centralized collection — a real and large risk reduction — and makes projects legally possible that otherwise aren't. It just isn't a privacy guarantee, and it's sold as one.
Isn't gradient inversion just a lab result?
The 2019 version needed favourable conditions; concede that. But those conditions are chosen by the server, not the client, and later work broke trained networks at higher resolution.
What noise level is enough?
No consensus number exists. Anyone offering one without stating the unit of privacy and the total budget over the whole run isn't answering.
Doesn't secure aggregation make poisoning undetectable?
Largely yes — that's the conflict slide. Partial answers exist; none is complete.
Who actually uses this?
Mobile keyboards were the original deployment and remain the canonical one — where the talk started. Cross-silo medical collaborations are the growing area.
OSI or FSF — who's right?
Give both, don't pick. FL is a concrete case where training data genuinely can't be shipped, which is exactly what they disagree about.
